On a recent Thursday, J. Galen Buckwalter drove 15 minutes from his home to the California Institute of Technology, steered his wheelchair into a laboratory and, as he had twice a week for two years, plugged in his brain.

More precisely, he allowed a researcher to attach wires to three metal knobs screwed into his skull like a cyberpunk mohawk. The wires connect to six chips that were implanted in his brain during a seven-hour craniotomy. They record his neural signals — the firing of his brain when he thinks, speaks or moves — and transmit them to a computer to be analyzed.

“Check this out,” said the researcher, a Ph.D. student named Sean Darcy, pointing to three colored lines vibrating on a monitor.

“Where’s that?” Buckwalter said.

“Motor,” Darcy said — meaning the chip implanted in Buckwalter’s motor cortex.

“That’s nice,” Buckwalter said, admiring the image.

Those neural signals may prove to be hugely valuable. Already, brain-computer interfaces have enabled people with paralysis to fly drones and helped people who have lost the ability to speak to communicate — using just their thoughts. Eventually, researchers say, they could allow the regulation of emotions such as pain or fear; the instant transfer of knowledge, such as a new language; or the merging of human consciousness with artificial intelligence.

Buckwalter, 70, was almost totally paralyzed from the chest down after a diving accident in his teens. He signed up for the study, conducted jointly by Caltech and the University of Southern California, because he wanted to make life better for others like him.

Also, he was curious. After a distinguished career as a research scientist at U.S.C., he had developed the matching algorithms that powered eHarmony, the dating website. He later started companies that tried to use data to understand human psychology.

Now he had a chance to get an up-close, never-before-seen look at the brain’s limits and potential. He imagined what he could do with the information Caltech would collect: run new experiments, supercharge his companies, learn more about himself and human resilience.

But when he asked the researchers for access to the data — after the craniotomy, after weeks of recovery, after hours upon hours in the lab — they said no.

The output from his brain belonged to the researchers, they said, not to him.

Today, the argument over who controls some of the most intimate information about us — what we buy, where we travel, whom we text — has become familiar, even tired. But little has been said about brain data. That is largely because that data has seemed like the stuff of science fiction, accessible only to scientists armed with the most specialized technology.

But that is quickly changing. Researchers and private companies are exploring other applications of brain-computer interfaces, including wearable headsets and caps. Elon Musk has a company, Neuralink, that has already implanted more than a dozen people, and he has said he envisions a world where people can upgrade themselves by merging with computers. Someday, information about how we think, what we fear and the essence of who we are could be as accessible as our shopping history on Amazon.

n 5 August 2013, a man and a woman ate a burger. Not just any burger: a world-historic burger, made from cattle cells grown in a laboratory by Mark Post, a professor of vascular physiology at Maastricht University in the Netherlands. The burger had taken two years to produce and cost €250,000 (about £210,000 at the time), money furnished by the Google co-founder Sergey Brin. Now it was being tasted in public for the first time at Riverside Studios in London, at an event attended by 200 members of the press and livestreamed to thousands.

To make this particular burger, Post had extracted stem cells from the shoulder muscle of a living cow, then cultivated them in plastic containers filled with a nutrient-rich supplement called foetal bovine serum, which is derived from cow foetuses. The cells eventually became 20,000 strands of tissue, which in turn became a single 140g patty. Post believed that beef produced this way had the potential to dramatically reduce greenhouse-gas emissions and slow the climate crisis. Raising cattle for consumption requires massive amounts of land and water – and massive amounts of feed grown at a high environmental cost. He projected that global demand for meat would soon outstrip our ability to produce it the old-fashioned way, at which point our food systems would become unsustainable. But that didn’t have to mean a mass recalibration of our consumption habits or a global shift away from red meat. Post and his team had found another way.

Standing at a blond-wood island on an otherwise empty soundstage, Post lifted the kind of stainless-steel cloche usually found on a room service cart to reveal his creation. The raw patty sat in a petri dish on a bare plate, where it shone wetly under the studio lights. With the ITV London anchor Nina Hossain presiding, a Cornish chef named Richard McGeown pan-fried the burger in sunflower oil and butter for two volunteer tasters: Josh Schonwald, the Chicago-based author of The Taste of Tomorrow: Dispatches from the Future of Food, and Hanni Rützler, an Austrian nutrition scientist. So precious was this meat that they were given just the one burger to share between them. It was served deconstructed, alongside a sesame-seed bun, lettuce and tomato – showmanship to sell the burger a bit, give it context. The patty itself was reddish brown, flat and perfectly circular. When Schonwald and Rützler cut it, the burger did not bleed.

The two of them ate with forks and knives, passing the plate, sawing off thoughtful bites. Chewing slowly. Afterwards, they rendered their verdicts. “I would say it is somewhere on the spectrum between a Boca Burger and McDonald’s,” Schonwald told a reporter from NBC. “The absence of fat makes a big difference. It has the texture, which I was not expecting. It was like an animal-protein cake.” Rützler noted the burger’s blandness to a reporter from Science. “There is room for improvement here,” she said. At the end of the event, about €113,000 worth of burger remained uneaten. But none of this stopped Post from predicting that meat cultivated in a lab would become a consumer product within the decade.

The event – the theatrical reveal, the PR spectacle – triggered a kind of gold rush. One hundred and forty companies sprang up in six continents, trying to replicate what Post had done with beef across a range of other species: chicken, pork, fish, even quail. Investment to the tune of $3bn (£2.2bn) flooded into the sector. True believers claimed that cellular agriculture would not only fight the climate crisis and global food insecurity but also slow the spread of zoonotic disease and antibiotic resistance. A company called Upside Foods touted its plans to “set the new standard for meat” and to “make meat a force of good in the world”. A company called Vow created an enormous woolly mammoth meatball, though no one was allowed to eat it, since the DNA came from a fossil and the company didn’t know whether it was actually fit for human consumption. The thinktank RethinkX predicted that cultivated meat would decimate the US beef industry by 2030, reducing demand for the conventional variety by a whopping 70%. As the organisation wrote, “We are already on the cusp of the deepest, fastest, most consequential disruption in food and agricultural production since the first domestication of plants and animals 10,000 years ago.”

nd yet, despite the frenzy, the meat itself never arrived. The challenges were numerous. There was the exorbitant upfront cost of building manufacturing facilities and the daunting amounts of energy required to run them. There was the difficulty of producing sufficient quantities of the stuff, at a price point low enough for people to buy. There was the lack of a highly skilled workforce trained to operate the new technology. There were rounds and rounds of inspections by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the US Department of Agriculture (USDA). There was even, occasionally, the unwanted appearance of rodent DNA in research samples, which happened to the companies Upside and Eat Just. After peaking in 2021, investment began to fall off. In 2023, the industry raised a measly $177m globally.

But it wasn’t only venture capitalists who had started to grow sceptical of cultivated meat. The technology soon became a target of conservatives. The state governments of Florida, Alabama, Mississippi, Indiana, Montana, Nebraska and Texas moved in quick succession to legislate against cultivated meat, passing laws that banned its sale, production, or both. The Florida governor Ron DeSantis made a media event out of signing that state’s law, posing for pictures in front of a blood-stained cutting board piled high with steaks. “Take your fake lab-grown meat elsewhere,” he said. “We’re not doing that in the state of Florida.” In 2024, the industrial biotech consultant Joel Stone told the New York Times that, if he had to put odds on it, the likelihood of the industry producing even 10% of the meat supply in the foreseeable future “would be zero”. Today, cultivated meat is available for purchase at just one store in the world: Huber’s Butchery in Singapore, where customers can buy packages of lab-grown chicken shreds from a manufacturer called GOOD Meat.

These developments were inconvenient for me, a person who became obsessed with the prospect of tasting cell-grown meat just as the possibility of ever doing so seemed to recede before my eyes. I was enthralled by the story of the meat, which seemed to contain so much of what defined this era of American life: dubious techno-optimism, bombastic culture-war posturing, carnival oddities like the mammoth meatball. It presented a snarl of competing motives, a mix of altruism and cold-blooded striving for capital (nothing can ever exist just for the common good; it has to make someone a billionaire). There was also the fact that I had been a vegetarian for almost 20 years.

The last meat I ever ate was a hotdog at Shea Stadium in New York in 2007. I don’t remember how it tasted – probably like every other hotdog I had eaten before. I washed it down with an exorbitantly priced but very cold Budweiser in one of those metal bottles. I quit eating meat after that, not because the hotdog had been disgusting but because I didn’t like the sensation of the inevitable bad bite, the periodic tangle with gristle or tendon or bone. Then my abstention grew into habit, and the habit was reinforced by other concerns: the catastrophic environmental consequences of industrial meat production; the creeping doubt, as I grow older, that I’m necessarily higher in the cosmic order than a chicken or a cow. But cultivated meat raised a compelling question. If you could subtract some of the downsides – make meat less destructive, engineer away its aesthetic flaws – what reason would there be not to eat it?

hat’s on your mind right now? You can probably answer this question without thinking too hard about it. Being aware of our own thought processes — a capacity called introspection — seems to come naturally to us.

Yet among philosophers and psychologists, introspection is a subject of active debate. Some think it’s closely related to consciousness. Others argue that it’s an elaborate illusion. It’s tricky to test theories of introspection in the lab — or to pin down a precise definition in the first place.

Now, after over a century of studying introspection in humans and animals, researchers are beginning to explore the phenomenon in unlikely new subjects — large language models. These systems, which are based on mathematical structures called neural networks, excel at cognitive tasks that until recently seemed exclusive to humans. Can they introspect as well?

In 2025, Jack Lindsey, a researcher at Anthropic, devised a way to test introspection in language models by injecting “thoughts” into their neural networks — in a kind of targeted digital neurosurgery. Lindsey’s experiment demonstrated that models could at least sometimes detect and describe these injections. It looked a lot like introspection.

Lindsey’s paper was deliberately limited in scope — it didn’t make broader claims about consciousness. (In particular, it did not imply that language models have subjective first-person experiences.) Even so, it caused a stir online. Researchers rushed to replicate Lindsey’s study with other models.

In the year since, a clearer picture has emerged. Some aspects of the phenomenon Lindsey observed have simpler explanations unrelated to introspection. Others have proved harder to explain away, but whether they should count as introspection remains hotly contested.

It’s a heady time for the field, as researchers devise real-world tests for questions that were once considered philosophical. “Given the state of the theoretical landscape, where no one agrees on anything,” Lindsey said, “I think that our best path forward is to do a lot of empirical science.”

Language models learn to generate fluent sentences by “training” on vast reams of human-authored text. That means if you ask them what they “think,” their answers may just mimic the kinds of responses found in their training data. “They are trained to talk like humans, and humans talk about their inner life all the time,” said Shauli Ravfogel, a language model researcher at New York University.

To avoid such pitfalls, researchers want to devise tests of introspection that can’t be gamed using outside information. Ideally, a language model will only pass these tests if it’s actually aware of its current internal state.

Lindsey set out to devise such a test. He’d been a graduate student in computational neuroscience, using neural networks as tools to model brain processes, when the world began to take notice of language models. “It started to feel like there’s just a lot of value in understanding them for their own sake,” he said. In 2024, after receiving his doctorate, he left academia to study the inner workings of language models at Anthropic.

Lindsey and his colleagues developed methods to identify patterns of activity in a language model’s “neurons” that encoded specific concepts. They could, for example, show that one pattern encoded the concept of “the Golden Gate Bridge,” while another encoded the concept of “inner conflict.” By artificially dialing up a specific pattern, they could steer the model’s outputs toward the corresponding concept.

The researchers showed off their technique in a public demo: For one day in May 2024, users of Anthropic’s language model Claude could interact with a bizarro “Golden Gate Bridge” version. The modified model proceeded to bring up the bridge in its responses to user queries on completely unrelated subjects. When one user asked Claude about the calorie content of a McDonald’s meal, for instance, the model replied, “The food with the highest calorie count at the Golden Gate Bridge is the Golden Gate Bridge itself, which contains around 1.6 million calories worth of steel cables.”

he idea of separating church and state – now entrenched in many modern liberal democracies – is in some ways strange. After all, governments must keep themselves informed and inform their citizens. That’s why they turn to the science of meteorology to predict weather patterns, to economics to understand patterns of growth and decline, and to the medical sciences to improve public health. So why wouldn’t governments also keep close alliances with religious institutions to know what’s going on with God?

Separating church and state appears strange from other perspectives as well. In the historical sweep of large-scale human societies, it’s a relative newcomer, often associated with the United States’ founding father Thomas Jefferson. We’re fans of Jefferson’s principle, one of us (Neil Van Leeuwen) being a philosophy professor who grew up in the Christian Reformed Church, and the other (Jacob Eisler) being a law professor who regularly attends synagogue. But Jefferson’s letter makes putting the principle into practice sound simpler than it is – as though governments could be entirely hands-off when it comes to people’s beliefs, or ‘opinions’. That, however, can’t be right: engaging with and shaping political, social and other kinds of beliefs is actually a core function of government. Public education guides what people believe about everything from mathematics to history to astronomy. Governments also shape beliefs for policy reasons. If fire department officials learn that people believe a building is safe when it isn’t, they will try to change what people believe about the building’s safety. Belief is also a category of legal interest. If a psychotherapist, for example, believes a patient is a danger to himself or others, the therapist is obligated by law to report that belief.

All this means that Jefferson’s ‘wall of separation’ can’t sensibly prohibit governments from intervening on beliefs of any type whatsoever. Rather, Jefferson – along with advocates of the principle who came after him – must have presupposed that there is something distinctive about religious beliefs that makes it reasonable to give them a different legal status.

But what is this distinctive thing? Is it that religious beliefs concern God? No, because Buddhism is a religion with its own beliefs, yet Buddhism has no supreme deity. Is it that religious beliefs concern the supernatural generally? No, because many superstitions that aren’t part of a religion concern the supernatural too. Is it that religious beliefs concern morality, as the biologist Stephen Jay Gould once suggested? No, because many religious beliefs aren’t about morality, and many moral beliefs aren’t religious. In any case, any system of government must presuppose some moral beliefs – eg, that murder is wrong – to be a government at all. These considerations might make one despair of articulating the psychological distinction of interest – what’s different about religious ‘belief’? – that’s needed to make sense of separating church and state in the first place.

Such attempts at characterising religious belief fail, we think, because they focus on belief contents, or what the believing is about. But since any given idea can be believed in a religious way or not, that won’t work. Our suggestion, then, is that religiously believing is a different attitude from ordinary matter-of-fact believing – a different way of relating to ideas – and that this is what’s distinctive about religious belief. In other words, sacralising an idea is different from just taking it to describe how things are. Furthermore, empirical research in cognitive science and anthropology of religion, along with accompanying philosophical analysis, can help reveal characteristic features of the difference in attitude we’re after. To have specific terms, we call that the difference between religious credence and factual belief. Understanding this difference, we’ll see, helps make sense of Jefferson’s wall of separation, and supports the rationale for separating church and state in liberal democracies more generally.

hatever they are, ‘beliefs’ come in different varieties. The word ‘belief’ is polysemous – meaning it can refer to a range of different-but- related things – and it subsumes a motley crew: from humdrum factual beliefs (say, about where the ice-cream store is) to superstitions (like those found in astrology), and from moral beliefs about murder to philosophical beliefs about how ‘all is one’. As mentioned earlier, beliefs can also be categorised by their contents (what is the belief about? Is it about politics? Or maybe about a probable scientific theory?) Or by the attitude they embody (how does a person relate to given ideas or contents?) Here, however, we focus on the differences between matter-of-fact believing and religious credence as distinct ways in which people process ideas. There are, of course, more varieties of belief-like attitudes. But we home in on factual belief and religious credence since the differences in those attitudes matter most to the psychology of separating church and state.

Ethnographies of religion, like the anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann’s When God Talks Back (2012), feature the devout talking of their ‘beliefs’ as a matter of choice. One of the congregants from the Vineyard Church whom Luhrmann interviewed told her: ‘There is always a choice to believe what it is.’ And a common theme in the Vineyard is choosing Jesus, which means choosing to believe in Jesus. Nor is choosing ‘belief’ unique to Christianity. People around the world switch religions for reasons of romance or for other social incentives, which means voluntarily choosing what religious credence to have.

One of the more interesting stories about Benjamin Franklin, told in his Autobiography, is his attempt to cultivate in himself 13 virtues. These were: temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility. He should strive, he wrote, to “imitate Jesus and Socrates.”

The final virtue on Franklin’s list, humility, was a late addition — a Quaker friend informed him that he was generally thought proud, and that this pride often came across in conversation. He endeavored to cure himself of this folly, including it in his plan for self-examination.

But it is easy to wonder, reading his account of his own life, how much Franklin really accomplished in seeking to eradicate pride from his self- conception. He “[could not] boast of much success,” he wrote, “in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but [he] had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it.” This paid dividends. His affected modesty made others more readily accept his ideas, and he felt less embarrassed when he was mistaken. The virtues, for the great empiricist Franklin, were worth cultivating in part because of the practical advantages they seemed to confer.

In studying and seeking to perfect the virtues, Franklin was joining a tradition that dates back to Aristotle. It was Aristotle who offered the most influential systematic study of virtue as a locus for moral thought. Virtue enjoyed a central role in Western thought for roughly two millennia afterward, and then began, in the 18th and 19th centuries, to fade; it was only in the second half of the 20th century, through the work of philosophers like Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre, that virtue reemerged, now under the aegis of “virtue ethics” — a primarily neo-Aristotelian project critical of the wayward bent of moral theorists of the time.

Decades have passed since their seminal contributions, and it would seem, in our age seemingly so bereft of it, that “virtue” is having its moment once again. Jay Caspian Kang writes in The New Yorker of the “death of shame.” William Thibeau connects MacIntyre’s notion of “characters,” or “marks worn by moral philosophies,” to the moral calculus of effective altruism.

In a time of transition, when technological change seems bound to rewrite the rules of sociality and conviviality, we are reaching for virtue as one buoy to help us navigate troubled waters. That grasping is nowhere more apparent than in AI alignment, where it looks like we have to teach the systems we are creating how to be “good.” So we are rediscovering, under turbulent conditions, virtue. But will it help us?

AI alignment has always started with the question: If, one day, we develop superintelligent machines, how do we ensure they do what we actually want? In the field’s prehistory, Norbert Wiener warned that we must ensure the “purpose put into the machine” is the purpose that we really desire. If a powerful AI takes shortcuts to achieve our goals, or misunderstands what those goals are, it might have unintended effects.

Wiener’s concern reflected a specific idea of what a machine’s “purpose” could be. In 1943, Wiener, along with Arturo Rosenblueth and Julian Bigelow, explained that it was meaningful to call some machines purposeful, in that they behaved so as to reach a target state and were corrected by negative feedback. A thermostat, for instance, is not conscious, but could be accurately described as “trying to” reach 68 degrees by checking the current temperature, comparing it with the target temperature, and taking some action to close the gap.

This teleological view was soon applied to AI systems that might have a goal more complex than that of a thermostat. In 1944, John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern argued that under some conditions, one can represent a person’s preferences with a utility function. Later, in 1950, Claude Shannon introduced the notion of an evaluation function to explain how a machine for playing chess might evaluate whether its board position was a winning or losing one.

Both of these ideas were absorbed into the emerging field of artificial intelligence over the following decades, particularly in reinforcement learning. An intelligent machine, then, could be seen as a system with a numerical objective, searching over possible actions for the highest- scoring approach. This optimization process, chasing a numerical score, misses anything outside of the direct objective.

In 1965, I.J. Good wrote of an “ultraintelligent machine” that could, because of its intellectual superiority to humans, design even better machines and beget an intelligence explosion. But Vernor Vinge, in 1993, did more to shape the intellectual world from which the modern field of alignment emerged in the early 2000s, centered on thinkers like Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky. They worried about what Vinge called the “Coming Technological Singularity” — the moment when these self- improving artificial intelligences would create an explosive technological transformation far beyond the ability of humans to understand or control. If — or, for Yudkowsky, when — such systems existed, the purpose we put into the machine would be of the utmost importance.

Early research on “Friendly AI” — a term Yudkowsky popularized in a 2001 essay — confronted a problem of motivational stability. In Yudkowsky’s picture, a “seed AI” would improve itself by recursively rewriting its own source code without human intervention, evolving into a human-equivalent or even superhuman intelligence by doing so. Because it would come to understand and revise the code and cognitive architecture through which it represented and pursued its goals, its designers could not be satisfied with giving the first version an acceptable motivational structure. That initial motivation — or whatever made it acceptable — would have to survive into the cognitive systems that followed. The worry was not that the machine would decide to become evil, but that its original purposes might be corrupted, discarded, or altered in meaning as it reconstructed the machinery that implemented them. Even if the seed AI valued human life, its successors might not.

In 2004, Yudkowsky developed a sophisticated account of what kinds of goals we might want to impart to an AI — assuming, of course, those values could be made to stick. A friendly superintelligence should pursue what he called the coherent extrapolated volition of humanity: the things that we would want as a species if we knew more, thought faster, were more like our ideal selves, and had more time to collectively reflect on our values.
