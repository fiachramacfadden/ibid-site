I Visited Big Bear Lake’s Famous Bald Eagles and Found Very Human Shenanigans

It was the middle of winter, the sad dark stretch when the sun seems to set before it rises, when the snow outside is dishwater gray, and I was handling it the way I always do: vegetating under a mound of pillows, poking at my glowing screen, praying that the internet would serve me something good — not the usual toxic fire hose of spam, slop, bots, gambling ads and phishing links.

Instantly, a new world filled my screen. At the bottom, dominating the foreground, was the nest: a big messy tangle of sticks and branches. Behind that, stretching off to infinity, was a panoramic view that looked too good to be true: green forest, blue water, purple mountains. It looked like a Renaissance painting of the Garden of Eden — the sort of sun-splashed paradise the word “sublime” was invented to describe.

The feed seemed to have a good microphone, because as soon as I clicked, all the noises of the forest came pouring into my house: songbirds chirping, ravens croaking, woodpeckers hammering out their overlapping drum rolls — and, over it all, the constant roar of an oceanic wind, a big crashing whoosh that thrashed the treetops, so loud and harsh it sounded like fire.

Why the dilettante is the anti-hero of our over-optimised age

Why, of all people, defend the dilettante? An amateur at best, a flighty rascal at worst: why take an interest in someone whose own interests are never more than a passing hobby? These are serious times – why take seriously someone for whom nothing is serious?

Merriam-Webster defines the dilettante as ‘a person having a superficial interest in an art or a branch of knowledge’. Encyclopedia.com calls the dilettante someone who cultivates an interest ‘without real commitment or knowledge’. Cambridge settles for someone whose understanding ‘is not very deep or serious’. None of it is flattering.

Clearly, the dilettante is no aspirational character. No university markets itself as training the next generation of dilettantes; no magazine runs a ‘dilettante of the year’ issue (except, perhaps, for the excellent Idler publication). He – for the dilettante is, historically and in popular imagination, so often a ‘he’ – is in essence an amateur, a role defined by wide interests and narrow expertise.

Quanta The Evolving Story of How Chemistry Became Biology

Around 300 million years into the existence of our solar system, conditions on Earth were right for a great chemical experiment to begin. Molecules entangled themselves in self-sustaining, self-reproducing loops. Over time, these systems became more complex. At some point, they had come to life.

Life invented new ways to move, and to capture, store, and use energy. It began to sense, respond to, and change its environment. It communicated, ate some of its neighbors and lived inside others, and formed complex, dynamic communities. Through cycles of birth, reproduction, and death; catastrophe; and bursts of evolution, it persisted and then thrived, taking over every corner of the planet.

Then, at some point, life gained a sense of itself, a reflective intelligence with the existential curiosity to ask: Where did we come from? For tens of thousands of years, we have pondered and probed the origins of life: Monks have meditated, philosophers have argued, lovers have mused, and parents have patiently answered their kids’ questions.
