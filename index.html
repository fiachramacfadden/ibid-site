On a recent Thursday, J. Galen Buckwalter drove 15 minutes from his home to the California Institute of Technology, steered his wheelchair into a laboratory and, as he had twice a week for two years, plugged in his brain.

More precisely, he allowed a researcher to attach wires to three metal knobs screwed into his skull like a cyberpunk mohawk. The wires connect to six chips that were implanted in his brain during a seven-hour craniotomy. They record his neural signals — the firing of his brain when he thinks, speaks or moves — and transmit them to a computer to be analyzed.

“Check this out,” said the researcher, a Ph.D. student named Sean Darcy, pointing to three colored lines vibrating on a monitor.

“Where’s that?” Buckwalter said.

“Motor,” Darcy said — meaning the chip implanted in Buckwalter’s motor cortex.

“That’s nice,” Buckwalter said, admiring the image.

Those neural signals may prove to be hugely valuable. Already, brain-computer interfaces have enabled people with paralysis to fly drones and helped people who have lost the ability to speak to communicate — using just their thoughts. Eventually, researchers say, they could allow the regulation of emotions such as pain or fear; the instant transfer of knowledge, such as a new language; or the merging of human consciousness with artificial intelligence.

Buckwalter, 70, was almost totally paralyzed from the chest down after a diving accident in his teens. He signed up for the study, conducted jointly by Caltech and the University of Southern California, because he wanted to make life better for others like him.

Also, he was curious. After a distinguished career as a research scientist at U.S.C., he had developed the matching algorithms that powered eHarmony, the dating website. He later started companies that tried to use data to understand human psychology.

Now he had a chance to get an up-close, never-before-seen look at the brain’s limits and potential. He imagined what he could do with the information Caltech would collect: run new experiments, supercharge his companies, learn more about himself and human resilience.

But when he asked the researchers for access to the data — after the craniotomy, after weeks of recovery, after hours upon hours in the lab — they said no.

The output from his brain belonged to the researchers, they said, not to him.

Today, the argument over who controls some of the most intimate information about us — what we buy, where we travel, whom we text — has become familiar, even tired. But little has been said about brain data. That is largely because that data has seemed like the stuff of science fiction, accessible only to scientists armed with the most specialized technology.

But that is quickly changing. Researchers and private companies are exploring other applications of brain-computer interfaces, including wearable headsets and caps. Elon Musk has a company, Neuralink, that has already implanted more than a dozen people, and he has said he envisions a world where people can upgrade themselves by merging with computers. Someday, information about how we think, what we fear and the essence of who we are could be as accessible as our shopping history on Amazon.

hen I was a kid, my mom called the stack of books on my nightstand a “smorgasbord.” People use a lot of food language when they talk about reading. We devour; we gulp. I like to “graze” as a reader, moving through multiple books simultaneously, dipping in and out according to my mood. Romantic metaphors, too, come in handy, alluding to the vagaries of appetite and compatibility and taste. I would say that I’m still not a monogamous reader. I play the field; I read around.

The current count of books I’m partway through is eighteen: eight print books, eight e- books, and two audiobooks. There’s some nonfiction in there: Into the Ice by Mark Synnott, Boom by Michael Shnayerson, and the audiobook of Entitled by Andrew Lownie, which, if you need to be convinced that Prince Andrew is one of the most odious people on the planet, boy do I have the book for you. The majority of my docket is taken up by novels, though. A galley of Emily St. John Mandel’s new book Exit Party is on my nightstand, as is Ian McEwan’s What We Can Know. (Bad title! I hate titles that are unmemorable strings of nonspecific words!) I’m in a race to finish Venetian Vespers by John Banville before Libby, the public library app, takes it back for maybe the fourth time. The Banville is short and enjoyable but has been a victim of the smorgasbord approach, never quite seizing priority. Also on my Libby shelf are, among others, Lovers XXX by Allie Rowbottom, The Original by Nell Stevens, and Villa Coco by Andrew Sean Greer. On my Bookshop.org app, I have The Remembered Soldier, a magnificent novel by the Dutch writer Anjet Daanje that is very long and that I ended up (happily) buying because I didn’t want to race Libby’s loan period.

I wasn’t an early adopter of e-books, partly because my first novel was published in 2012, at the height of e-book doomerism, when everyone was saying print books were soon to be a thing of the past and e-books were the enemy. (Interestingly—at least to me —the ratio of print copies to digital has gone up for each of my three successive books, with only that first one selling more e-books than physical.) The Kindle launched in 2007, but I didn’t have an e-reader until 2013, when I got a Kobo in a gift bag. I liked it, but the process of acquiring e-books and getting them on the device was cumbersome, and, anyway, within a year I left it on an airplane.

During the pandemic, I found myself meandering back toward e-books. I’d had an iPad for a while but didn’t really know what to do with it until a friend told me about Libby. Somehow I’d forgotten libraries existed, and I didn’t have a card for the Los Angeles system, even though I’d lived in the city for six years by then. I signed up for one online, downloaded the app, and everything changed. I didn’t even have to leave my house, which I wasn’t supposed to do anyway.

I’d never lost the habit of reading in bed at night, but my intake of books had slowed dramatically over the years, probably starting in college. I was an English lit major, so reading was work. Then writing became my job in my mid-20s, and that professionalism wore away any playfulness that was left in reading. I’d gotten timid and precious in my choices, only buying books I felt confident I would enjoy but then often not enjoying them after all, at which point they would linger, stubbornly material, in my house until I rounded up a box or two to donate. With Libby, I could be capricious again. Every time I came across an intriguing mention of a book or someone recommended one, I placed a hold. I would then immediately and completely forget about the hold until, days, weeks, or months later, some vaguely recalled title appeared on my iPad, a gift from my past self. Something about the convenience and low stakes of Libby got me reading like I hadn’t in years. I became a filter-feeder, opening my maw to take in books indiscriminately, unbothered by the ones that must be spat out. In 2020 I read 60 books; last year I read 92. I started but didn’t finish dozens more.

The dream, though, even for a nonmonogamist like myself, is The One, the book that makes all others disappear, that enchants you completely and exclusively until the last page boots you back out into the world. That feeling of absorption is one I crave, and I’ve experienced it many, many times, courtesy of books of all different genres and the whole spectrum of high- to low-browness. Last week it was the audiobook of Patrick Radden Keefe’s London Falling. In January it was a re-read of Ariel Levy’s memoir The Rules Do Not Apply. Lately I’ve been immersed in Michelle Huneven’s back catalog. Possibly the most delicious experiences of The One emerge from a perfect alchemy of time, place, and book, like when I found an orange and white Penguin edition of The Secret History in a used bookshop in Bali in 2011 and, having somehow never heard of it, bought it on the basis of the title alone. Or when I read Persuasion on my phone in a tent in the Swedish Arctic. Or when, traveling alone in New Zealand, I read all the Twilight books in rapid succession, and upon finishing the last one, felt like I’d woken up in a bus stop, disheveled and hungover and utterly mystified as to how I’d gotten there.

The One is not singular but takes infinite forms. There is no heartbreak when you move on. In fact, the pleasure is additive. A writer friend reminded me of this once when I was expressing disgruntled envy about the reception of someone else’s book. “No one ever finishes a good book and says, ‘Now I’ll never read another book again,’” she said.

Free love prevails at the smorgasbord. And, I mean, there’s a title.

On a sunny Saturday morning earlier this year, I was driving my two daughters to the playground through patchy phone signal when a voicemail notification appeared. Pressing play impatiently, an upbeat voice with a north of England accent bellowed through the car’s loudspeaker: “Hi Ed. I’ve just been flicking through The Times, and I’ve seen you’ve been chosen as their book of the week. Bloody well done, my friend! I am super proud.”

I was stunned. My book, A Hateful Decision, about Churchill’s attack on the French navy during the Second World War, had just been published. Being named The Times Book of the Week was, without question, the highlight of my writing career. But the way this news was delivered felt profoundly weird. The man who had congratulated me— referring to me as his “friend” with such sincerity—was Robin Garbutt, and he’d called from Wealstun Prison, where he was, and still is, serving a life sentence for the murder of his wife, Diana. She was killed in her bed, beaten so brutally that her face was barely recognizable by the time the police got there.

In 2021, I was researching a possible sequel to my first book, a biography of Ian Fleming, the creator of James Bond. While at the National Archives in West London, partly through a filing error and partly through my own boredom, I found myself looking at recent criminal case files rather than the wartime naval intelligence records I had come for. One caught my eye immediately: Diana Garbutt, a woman murdered in the flat above the post office she ran with her husband Robin in the North Yorkshire village of Melsonby.

The only available document for her murder was the judge’s summing up, and it was damning. Robin had called the emergency services on the morning of March 23, 2010, saying their post office had been robbed at gunpoint. After the perpetrators left, he claimed he had run upstairs to find Diana dead. But no one in the village had seen any evidence of the robbery, even though it was a busy morning. The paramedics who first attended Diana noted that she had rigor mortis—a sign she had died many hours before Robin claimed the robbery took place. It appeared Robin had invented the robbery to cover his own murderous tracks. The descriptions of the wounds she had sustained in her final moments of life were horrific. As I read on, I felt nothing but contempt for her husband. The judge made it clear to the jury that he believed Robin to be a killer. Ten of the 12 jurors agreed, and Robin was found guilty by a majority verdict.

When I got home from the archives, I looked up the case online, curious to know what had become of Robin. There was a strange sense of relief to learn he was still behind bars. But as I scanned through the search results, something made me double-take: a website dedicated to Robin Garbutt. What kind of convicted murderer has his own website? It turned out his family had set one up, maintaining throughout that he was innocent of killing his wife. I was, of course, skeptical; a lot of people in prison claim they should never have been convicted. But what struck me about Robin’s website was the list of supporters: family, childhood friends, and a remarkable number of Melsonby residents, a village of fewer than 800 people.

Getting in touch with Robin was surprisingly straightforward. The “contact us” section of his website directed me to his friend Jane Metcalfe—a proud woman from the same part of the country as Robin, and someone who would rather use ten words when one would suffice. She was desperate for more media attention for the case. A new author was hardly what she had in mind, but she graciously stuck with me, suggesting I write to Robin directly. We started to exchange letters through a system called eMates. I would type a letter to Robin, which would be printed and delivered to his cell. For an extra 26 pence, I could include a response sheet, on which he would write a reply by hand.

Over the next six months of writing to each other, Robin would instruct Jane to send me documents from his case. I started to build up my own archive of police reports, trial transcripts, and witness statements, all arriving in hard copy through the post. Thanks to years of reading and watching fictional detectives like Sherlock Holmes and Miss Marple, a surprising amount of the prosecution documents made sense to me. But there was one element I still couldn’t understand: What type of person could murder their wife in such a merciless way? For someone painted as so gentle by those who knew him, it just did not add up. Perhaps he was a psychopath? That possibility scared me the most. At the same time, I was desperate to find out more—to the point I thought I might scrap Ian Fleming and write a book about the murder instead.

When I broached the topic with Robin in one of my many letters to him, he suggested we should meet. I jumped at the chance, due to curiosity as much as anything else. As far as I was aware, I had never met a convicted murderer. I had certainly never set foot in a prison. As a director in a large corporation, it was a far cry from the air-conditioned London offices I was used to.

Jane was instrumental in the coordination, kindly collecting me from York Station, where she brushed aside my outstretched hand in favor of a warm bear hug. The half- hour drive to Wealstun Prison was conducted at such speed I was no longer frightened by the prospect of entering a high-security prison so much as making it there in one piece. Jane did not look at the road nearly as much as I would have liked, either, but in fairness maybe she did not need to—she had made this journey so many times before. Her red Ford Fiesta told its own story: a flyer tucked in the windscreen declaring Robin’s conviction the country’s worst miscarriage of justice, the back seat buried under the paperwork of his various appeals. By the time we pulled up outside the prison, I had been given the full case for Robin’s innocence at considerable volume. Then Jane drove off and left me alone in front of a great fence that seemed to swallow the sky.

One of the more interesting stories about Benjamin Franklin, told in his Autobiography, is his attempt to cultivate in himself 13 virtues. These were: temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility. He should strive, he wrote, to “imitate Jesus and Socrates.”

The final virtue on Franklin’s list, humility, was a late addition — a Quaker friend informed him that he was generally thought proud, and that this pride often came across in conversation. He endeavored to cure himself of this folly, including it in his plan for self-examination.

But it is easy to wonder, reading his account of his own life, how much Franklin really accomplished in seeking to eradicate pride from his self- conception. He “[could not] boast of much success,” he wrote, “in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but [he] had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it.” This paid dividends. His affected modesty made others more readily accept his ideas, and he felt less embarrassed when he was mistaken. The virtues, for the great empiricist Franklin, were worth cultivating in part because of the practical advantages they seemed to confer.

In studying and seeking to perfect the virtues, Franklin was joining a tradition that dates back to Aristotle. It was Aristotle who offered the most influential systematic study of virtue as a locus for moral thought. Virtue enjoyed a central role in Western thought for roughly two millennia afterward, and then began, in the 18th and 19th centuries, to fade; it was only in the second half of the 20th century, through the work of philosophers like Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre, that virtue reemerged, now under the aegis of “virtue ethics” — a primarily neo-Aristotelian project critical of the wayward bent of moral theorists of the time.

Decades have passed since their seminal contributions, and it would seem, in our age seemingly so bereft of it, that “virtue” is having its moment once again. Jay Caspian Kang writes in The New Yorker of the “death of shame.” William Thibeau connects MacIntyre’s notion of “characters,” or “marks worn by moral philosophies,” to the moral calculus of effective altruism.

In a time of transition, when technological change seems bound to rewrite the rules of sociality and conviviality, we are reaching for virtue as one buoy to help us navigate troubled waters. That grasping is nowhere more apparent than in AI alignment, where it looks like we have to teach the systems we are creating how to be “good.” So we are rediscovering, under turbulent conditions, virtue. But will it help us?

AI alignment has always started with the question: If, one day, we develop superintelligent machines, how do we ensure they do what we actually want? In the field’s prehistory, Norbert Wiener warned that we must ensure the “purpose put into the machine” is the purpose that we really desire. If a powerful AI takes shortcuts to achieve our goals, or misunderstands what those goals are, it might have unintended effects.

Wiener’s concern reflected a specific idea of what a machine’s “purpose” could be. In 1943, Wiener, along with Arturo Rosenblueth and Julian Bigelow, explained that it was meaningful to call some machines purposeful, in that they behaved so as to reach a target state and were corrected by negative feedback. A thermostat, for instance, is not conscious, but could be accurately described as “trying to” reach 68 degrees by checking the current temperature, comparing it with the target temperature, and taking some action to close the gap.

This teleological view was soon applied to AI systems that might have a goal more complex than that of a thermostat. In 1944, John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern argued that under some conditions, one can represent a person’s preferences with a utility function. Later, in 1950, Claude Shannon introduced the notion of an evaluation function to explain how a machine for playing chess might evaluate whether its board position was a winning or losing one.

Both of these ideas were absorbed into the emerging field of artificial intelligence over the following decades, particularly in reinforcement learning. An intelligent machine, then, could be seen as a system with a numerical objective, searching over possible actions for the highest- scoring approach. This optimization process, chasing a numerical score, misses anything outside of the direct objective.

In 1965, I.J. Good wrote of an “ultraintelligent machine” that could, because of its intellectual superiority to humans, design even better machines and beget an intelligence explosion. But Vernor Vinge, in 1993, did more to shape the intellectual world from which the modern field of alignment emerged in the early 2000s, centered on thinkers like Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky. They worried about what Vinge called the “Coming Technological Singularity” — the moment when these self- improving artificial intelligences would create an explosive technological transformation far beyond the ability of humans to understand or control. If — or, for Yudkowsky, when — such systems existed, the purpose we put into the machine would be of the utmost importance.

Early research on “Friendly AI” — a term Yudkowsky popularized in a 2001 essay — confronted a problem of motivational stability. In Yudkowsky’s picture, a “seed AI” would improve itself by recursively rewriting its own source code without human intervention, evolving into a human-equivalent or even superhuman intelligence by doing so. Because it would come to understand and revise the code and cognitive architecture through which it represented and pursued its goals, its designers could not be satisfied with giving the first version an acceptable motivational structure. That initial motivation — or whatever made it acceptable — would have to survive into the cognitive systems that followed. The worry was not that the machine would decide to become evil, but that its original purposes might be corrupted, discarded, or altered in meaning as it reconstructed the machinery that implemented them. Even if the seed AI valued human life, its successors might not.

In 2004, Yudkowsky developed a sophisticated account of what kinds of goals we might want to impart to an AI — assuming, of course, those values could be made to stick. A friendly superintelligence should pursue what he called the coherent extrapolated volition of humanity: the things that we would want as a species if we knew more, thought faster, were more like our ideal selves, and had more time to collectively reflect on our values.
